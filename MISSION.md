# Mission

## Instructions for the reader

Paste the numbered claims below into your preferred LLM. Ask it to explain each claim in its own words, compare it against your own field or discipline, and identify which claim seems weakest or most contestable to you. If something comes back that changes our view (or yours), we'd like to hear it.

## Claims

1. Legal instruments (contracts, policies, licenses, terms of service) are treated today as monolithic documents, but they are increasingly assembled from reusable parts: clauses, definitions, precedents, boilerplate. The monolithic form obscures a modular reality.
2. Making that modularity explicit, treating legal text the way software treats code, as versioned, composable, forkable modules with dependency graphs, unlocks properties monolithic documents cannot deliver: reuse across jurisdictions, traceability of authority, and machine-tractable composition. Software solved a version of this problem decades ago; the migration of those tools into normative text is overdue.
3. Most of the boilerplate in commercial and legal transacting is a public good masquerading as private drafting. A shared, open library of components lets parties assemble transactions from a common substrate while keeping their bespoke deal points to themselves.
4. Composability at the human-legible level, not at the machine-token level, is what matters. A component a signer cannot actually read is not a component anyone can meaningfully agree to. Prose remains the interface between people and their commitments.
5. AI has a natural role here, but not the obvious one. The strongest contribution LLMs can make to contracting is not generating end-to-end text; that concentrates power in the model and defeats the intelligibility on which real commitment depends. It is helping produce, refine, connect, and translate modular components that remain the durable, auditable unit.
6. The system that curates and evolves these components must be multipolar, open, and free. Inconsistent efforts across languages, jurisdictions, and communities converge through use rather than through central coordination, with LLMs acting as bridges between forks.
7. CommonAccord has been building this substrate for more than a decade: a public, open-source library of prose objects that treats legal text as code, with the tooling and governance model to match. The question is no longer whether this is possible. It is who will use it, and for what.
